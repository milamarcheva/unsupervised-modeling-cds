To examine the effect of repeated exposure, we compare the HMM baseline after one, five, ten, and twenty complete passes through the training corpus. For each language, the additional results are obtained from checkpoints of a single twenty-pass run, retaining the corpus, state inventory, input order, and learning settings of the original one-pass baseline. English uses ten hidden states and Bulgarian eight. Online EM uses minibatches of one utterance, with the existing step-size reduction power of $0.5$ and offset of $2$. The update counter continues across passes. The input files are already shuffled; their order is retained on subsequent passes. No category-specific priming or gradual unlocking is applied.

The start, transition, and emission distributions are initialised close to uniform, with small random perturbations to break symmetry between latent states. We preserve the original toolkit settings: random seed $1$, noise parameter $\epsilon=0.001$, and initialisation smoothing $c=0.01$. Within each multinomial block, the implementation generates weights $w_i=(1+u_i)^{\epsilon}+c$, where $u_i\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1)$, and normalises them to probabilities. This follows the neutral-initialisation-with-noise rationale of \citet[Section~4, footnote~3]{liang-klein-2009-online}, without providing category-specific lexical anchors. The toolkit's exact transformation differs from the exponential expression printed in that paper; the implementation is retained here to preserve comparability with the original baseline. No additional smoothing is applied in the learning updates.

The original one-pass scores are retained. Before subsequent checkpoints are included, the first-pass predictions of each new run are checked for identical gold-category/cluster contingency counts and evaluation scores relative to the corresponding saved baseline. Training $\log Z$ is the primary metric, using the same quantity recorded as \texttt{train.logZ} for the original baselines. For each checkpoint, this is the token-normalised score accumulated during its final online-EM pass, while the parameters are updated; it is not a separate evaluation of the training corpus with the final parameters held fixed. The first-pass training scores are also checked against the saved baselines at the precision recorded by the toolkit. Predictions are additionally evaluated on the same annotated utterances using many-to-one accuracy, one-to-one accuracy, and variation of information. The comparison uses one fixed initialisation seed per language and therefore measures the effect of additional passes under that initialisation, rather than variability across random restarts.
